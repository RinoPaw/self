# Subject Ontology Comparative Audit — Field, Bearer, Primitive

> Date: 2026-10-05
>
> Status: candidate literature audit. Not authority until synthesis update.
>
> Target: compare three families of numerical subject identity on the same explananda rather than asking only whether consciousness is unified.

## 1. Families

### F — Phenomenal-field / bundle subject

A subject is constituted or individuated by a maximal organization of phenomenally unified experiences.

Core idea:

\[
Subject(S) \approx MaximalPhenomenalOrganization(S).
\]

### B — Causal / organismic bearer

A subject is identified primarily with an organism, nervous system, causal-functional system, or embodied bearer. Phenomenal unity is a property of the bearer, not necessarily its identity condition.

Core idea:

\[
SameBearer \not\Rightarrow NecessarilyOnePhenomenalField.
\]

### P — Primitive bearer

A subject is a numerically primitive owner of experience. Ownership is fundamental and need not be reduced to phenomenal, biological, functional, or causal relations.

Core idea:

\[
OwnedBy(e,S)
\]

is metaphysically basic.

## 2. Shared explananda

The comparison uses five tests:

1. **Ownership** — what makes these experiences experiences of this one subject?
2. **Split brain** — what happens when simultaneous experiential organization becomes strongly disconnected?
3. **Duplication / fission** — what fixes subject count when one system duplicates or branches?
4. **Overlap / combination** — can local subjects share experiences or compose a larger subject?
5. **Universal-I** — what would make all experiences belong to one numerical subject?

## 3. Ownership

### F

Best explanatory fit. Ownership is not an extra relation laid on top of experience; it is realized by membership in one maximal phenomenal organization.

Advantage:

\[
WhySameSubject? \to SamePhenomenalWhole.
\]

Cost: if unity is graded, intransitive, or overlapping, subject boundaries can also be graded, indeterminate, or overlapping. Schwitzgebel–Nelson make this cost explicit rather than treating it as incoherent.

### B

Ownership is inherited from bearer identity:

\[
ExperienceOf(e,S) \because e\text{ is realized in bearer }S.
\]

This works well for ordinary embodied cases. But it changes the explanandum: it explains why an experience belongs to an organism/system, while the question at issue is why disconnected phenomenal fields count as one **experiential subject**.

The current SEP split-brain survey explicitly distinguishes the structure of consciousness from the number/identity of subjects. One animal/body therefore does not settle one experiential subject.

### P

Ownership is direct by stipulation: experiences belong to S because S is their primitive bearer.

This is maximally flexible and guarantees numerical determinacy. But it provides the least internal criterion for comparing candidate assignments. If the same disconnected experiences could coherently be assigned to one primitive subject, two primitive subjects, or a universal primitive subject, further facts are needed to choose among assignments.

## 4. Split brain

The current unity literature treats split brain as an open dispute among one-stream, two-stream, switching, partial-stream, and hybrid models. Crucially, even accepting two streams does not alone settle subject count. citeturn996610search0

### F

If two maximally disconnected phenomenal organizations exist, two subjects follow naturally. Schechter-style two-subject interpretations fit this family especially well.

If partial unity obtains, F can model partial/overlapping subject structure rather than forcing exactly one or two.

### B

Strongest counterexample to F: a theorist can keep one bearer fixed across experiential disunity. This is metaphysically coherent enough to block a theorem.

But the view must explain why bearer identity, rather than the apparent multiplication of simultaneous first-person perspectives, controls experiential subject count.

### P

Can simply stipulate that one primitive subject persists through disunity. Again, coherence is easy; explanatory discrimination is difficult.

## 5. Duplication and fission

### F

Pre-duplication unity can branch into two successor phenomenal organizations. This makes numerical identity path-sensitive and naturally permits fission without a unique post-fission continuer.

Advantage: tracks the experiential structure actually created.

Cost: strict identity may be replaced by continuity/descendance relations.

### B

Physical duplication produces two bearers after duplication. Ordinary organismic/causal criteria therefore give a clean post-duplication plurality.

However, exact fission cases reveal that bearer continuity alone may fail to select a unique successor. The view inherits familiar personal-identity branching problems rather than solving them.

### P

A primitive bearer can select one continuer, split, duplicate, or cease depending on additional primitive laws. This makes the model unconstrained unless the laws are independently motivated.

## 6. Overlap and combination

### F

Roelofs-style between-subject unity and experience sharing show that phenomenal organization need not respect organism boundaries. This is an advantage for generality but creates non-classical subject topology.

Mørch supplies pressure against overlapping consciousness, especially on deflationary bundle approaches; Schwitzgebel–Nelson show that partial/intransitive unity can nevertheless motivate non-integer or indeterminate subject counts.

Osborn's composite-subject work blocks the cheap inference:

\[
ManyLocalSubjects \Rightarrow NoLargerSubject.
\]

So F can rule out Universal-I only if there is no actual global phenomenal connective structure.

### B

Bearer theories resist arbitrary overlap because biological/causal systems normally have relatively crisp boundaries. This is a practical virtue.

But higher-order causal systems, social systems, nested organisms, and distributed cognition reopen the boundary question. A mere appeal to causal integration needs a threshold rule, and threshold rules risk becoming conventional or theory-relative.

### P

Overlap and combination are whatever the primitive subject facts say they are. This gives modal freedom but little predictive constraint.

## 7. Universal-I stress test

### F: strongest locality pressure

A phenomenally constituted Universal-I must exhibit global phenomenal connectivity. If local fields are maximally disconnected, one universal phenomenal subject is not available.

\[
PhenomenalIndividuation + GlobalDisconnection
\Rightarrow
\neg UniversalI_{phenomenal}.
\]

This is the strongest current anti-Universal-I result.

### B: universal bearer requires a bearer candidate

An organismic/causal view cannot simply jump from many local organisms to one universal subject. It would need a single causal/functional system whose identity genuinely subsumes the local bearers and explains ownership across them.

The universe as a whole is not automatically such a bearer merely because it causally contains all organisms.

So bearer theories do not support Universal-I cheaply; they make it an empirical/metaphysical system-individuation claim.

### P: strongest Universal-I refuge

Primitive subjecthood provides the cleanest universalist escape:

\[
\exists!I_U\;\forall e\;OwnedBy(e,I_U)
\]

can be fundamental.

But the price is severe under comparison: the same formal move could equally posit many primitive subjects. Without an independent principle, primitive universalism does not explain why one assignment is correct.

## 8. Comparative table

| Test | Phenomenal field | Causal/organismic bearer | Primitive bearer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ownership explanation | strongest internal explanation | good embodied anchoring, weaker experiential criterion | fundamental but brute |
| Split brain | naturally tracks disunity/partiality | can preserve one bearer; owes subject-count bridge | can preserve anything by primitive fact |
| Duplication/fission | tracks branching organization | clear after duplication, hard exact fission | needs primitive continuation law |
| Overlap/combination | expressive but topologically messy | crisper local boundaries, threshold problem | unconstrained |
| Universal-I | requires global phenomenal unity | requires one genuine global bearer/system | easiest to state, hardest to discriminate |

## 9. Main result

No family wins deductively. But they do not have equal explanatory burdens.

The comparison suggests:

\[
\boxed{
PhenomenalFieldView\ has\ the\ strongest\ ownership\ explanation;
BearerView\ has\ the\ strongest\ ordinary\ embodiment\ anchor;
PrimitiveView\ has\ the\ weakest\ discrimination\ power.
}
\]

For the project's locality problem, this produces a new pressure principle:

### BDP — Bearer Discrimination Principle

If a theory claims that one numerical subject spans phenomenally disconnected fields, it must supply an independently motivated bearer-identity fact that distinguishes that one-subject assignment from rival many-subject assignments.

\[
\boxed{
DisconnectedFields + OneSubjectClaim
\Rightarrow
IndependentBearerDiscriminator.
}
\]

Phenomenal-field theories satisfy the discriminator via phenomenal organization but deny one subject across maximal disconnection. Causal/organismic theories can satisfy it through a genuine system identity, but must show why that system is the relevant subject bearer. Primitive theories currently satisfy it only by primitive stipulation.

## 10. Consequence for Universal-I

Universal-I now faces a three-horn burden:

\[
UniversalI \Rightarrow
\begin{cases}
GlobalPhenomenalConnectivity, & \text{field ontology};\\
OneIndependentlyUnifiedGlobalBearer, & \text{causal/organismic ontology};\\
PrimitiveUniversalBearer + AssignmentPrinciple, & \text{primitive ontology}.
\end{cases}
\]

This is stronger than the earlier two-horn PFIP formulation because it separates a serious extra-phenomenal bearer theory from bare primitive ownership.

## 11. Current verdict

The question has shifted from

\[
DoesSameSubjectRequireUnity?
\]

to

\[
\boxed{
What\ independently\ discriminates\ one\ subject\ assignment\ from\ rival\ assignments?
}
\]

That is the next useful locality criterion. A Universal-I is not refuted, but it must now identify the specific discriminator that turns universal ownership into more than relabelling all experiences with one variable.
